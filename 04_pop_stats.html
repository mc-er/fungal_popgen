Session 4: Population Statistics

Introduction

A variant call format (VCF) is a type of file that stores all the information regarding variant and non-variant sites of individuals mapped to a reference assembly. This assembly can be a single chromosome, or a collection of scaffolds- there really is no limit!

This morning we learned to read a VCF and judge the data behind it. This afternoon we start asking questions of one: how variable is it, how is that variation distributed along a chromosome, and how is it partitioned between populations.

NotePlan for the session
  1. Get your group’s scaffold and set up the metadata you will need for every plot
  2. Assess the quality of the VCF and decide how to filter it
  3. Calculate allele frequencies, nucleotide diversity, Tajima’s D and Fst
  4. Plot the results and work out what they are telling you

The commands are the quick part. Deciding what the numbers mean is the session.

ImportantWe change dataset here

This morning’s data was silver birch — a diploid tree, assessed to learn how to read pipeline output. From here to the end of the course we work on a forest fungus instead: haploid, smaller, and supplied with the population metadata that these statistics require.

The filtering reasoning you agreed on before lunch carries over. The thresholds themselves you will have to think about again, on a different organism.

Tools available

There are a multitude of tools available for the analysis of VCFs. It is almost entirely up to the researcher as to how they wish to interact with these files. Some bioinformaticians prefer to analyse files in the CLI and only plot the final data in R or python, while others prefer to do everything with R packages. For this part of the workshop, I will show you bits of both aspects.

One popular R package for VCF analyses is vcfR.

To plot data, we will be using ggplot2, and some functions of the dplyr package.

ImportantThe commands on this page are deliberately incomplete

You will notice that the commands below look like this:

vcftools --vcf --site-depth

The input file name, the output name and in several cases a window or bin size are missing on purpose. Filling them in is the exercise: you are expected to read the VCFtools manual, work out which arguments each option takes, and decide what to call the output.

A useful habit while you do that: --out sets the output prefix, and VCFtools chooses the extension itself according to what you asked for. Knowing which extension to expect tells you whether the command did what you thought.

NoteWhat facet_wrap buys you here

facet_wrap(~Location, scales = "free_x") splits one crowded plot into one small panel per location, each with its own x axis. With twenty-five populations on a single axis the labels are unreadable and nothing is comparable; split by location, you can see at a glance whether, say, the Norwegian samples were sequenced more deeply than the Finnish ones.

That is not a cosmetic question. Systematic differences in depth between the groups you intend to compare are a competing explanation for any difference you later find between them — the same point we made about the birch samples this morning, now with your own data.

Assessing quality of the VCF

We have a special version of VCFtools that supports haploid data (https://github.com/jydu/vcftools). Everything remains the same as the original version of VCFtools, with added support for haploid datasets that becomes important for some of the summary statistics. Have a look at the VCFtools manual to see the range of possible functions within this software (https://vcftools.sourceforge.net/man_latest.html).

WarningWhich binary to run, and when it matters

This is the point we flagged this morning, arriving in practice. Our fungus carries one copy of each chromosome, but a VCF records genotypes in a format designed for diploids, and statistics that are defined in terms of genotype counts therefore need to know which they are looking at.

Depth and quality metrics do not care about ploidy — they count reads and read qualities. Anything derived from allele or genotype counts does: allele frequencies, nucleotide diversity, Tajima’s D, Fst, and anything involving Hardy–Weinberg.

Unless you are told otherwise, use the haploid-aware build for everything on this page, and be able to say which of your numbers would have changed if you had not.

In VCFtools, it is important to remember to use the –recode option if you would like to output a new VCF that is filtered or contains a subset of positions or individuals. –out only sets the name of the output file. Use concise names so that it is easy for you to see what’s in the output.

Let us start by having a look at the sequencing depth of various aspects of the VCF. From the manual, figure out what each function means. Use less to see what’s in each of these output files.

vcftools-haploid --vcf --depth 
vcftools --vcf --site-depth 
vcftools --vcf  --site-mean-depth

Next, we want to see what the quality per site is

vcftools --vcf --site-quality

I prefer analysing these outputs in R. From there, decide on upper and lower boundaries to filter the variants on your scaffold. Justify why you think these filtering criteria are suitable.

The extension tells you what you are looking at. These are the ones you will meet this afternoon:

All of them also write a .log, which records exactly what the command did and how many sites survived. Read it. “After filtering, kept N out of a possible M Sites” is the single most informative line VCFtools prints, and it is how you catch a filter that removed far more — or far less — than you intended.

NoteAlso worth computing before you filter

Two of the thresholds agreed this morning were about missing data, and you cannot apply those without measuring them first:

vcftools --vcf --missing-indv
vcftools --vcf --missing-site

An individual missing most of its genotypes contributes almost nothing except noise, and is a candidate for removal with --remove-indv. A site missing in half the individuals is a candidate for removal with --max-missing.

Once you have decided, your filters go in one command rather than several, so that the log records the whole decision in one place:

vcftools --vcf --minDP --minQ --max-missing --recode --recode-INFO-all --out

--recode-INFO-all is worth knowing about: a plain --recode drops the INFO fields from the output VCF, which is a surprise you only want to have once.

Statistics per Chromosome

Keep only the scaffold your group is working on in your folder

vcftools --vcf --chr --recode

For downstream analyses, we need to remove all indels from the VCF

vcftools --vcf --remove-indels --recode

For most analyses, we only consider bi-allelic sites

vcftools --vcf --min-alleles 2 --max-alleles 2 --recode
NoteWhy remove indels, and why only two alleles?

Neither of these is housekeeping — both are assumptions you are choosing to make.

Indels are harder to call correctly from short reads than substitutions, because they require the aligner to have placed a gap in exactly the right position. In repetitive sequence it frequently does not. Most of the statistics below are also defined for single-nucleotide polymorphisms, so an indel in the input is at best ignored and at worst mishandled.

Bi-allelic sites are sites with exactly one alternative allele. Tajima’s D, Fst and the site frequency spectrum are all formulated in terms of two alleles at a site, so sites with three or more are excluded rather than approximated. Genuine tri-allelic sites exist, but at the depth and sample size we have, a third allele is more often a mapping artefact than biology.

Both filters make your data cleaner and your dataset smaller. Note in your log how many sites each one removed.

Look at allele frequency

vcftools --vcf --freq

Tajima’s D in bins

vcftools --vcf --TajimaD
NoteWhat Tajima’s D is actually comparing

Tajima’s D contrasts two different ways of estimating the same quantity — the amount of genetic variation you would expect in a population of constant size that is not under selection.

One estimate comes from the number of variable sites, which is sensitive to rare variants. The other comes from the average number of differences between pairs of individuals, which is dominated by common variants. Under the neutral, constant-size model the two agree and D is around zero. When they disagree, the sign tells you how:

  • Negative D — an excess of rare variants relative to common ones. Consistent with a population that has expanded (new mutations have not had time to become common), or with a selective sweep having recently removed variation and new mutations beginning to accumulate.
  • Positive D — a deficit of rare variants, or an excess of intermediate-frequency ones. Consistent with a population contraction, with structure that has been pooled together, or with balancing selection maintaining two alleles.

Two cautions that matter more than the interpretation. First, demography affects the whole genome, while selection affects particular regions — so a genome-wide shift in D is a demographic story, and only an outlier region against that background is a candidate for selection. With one scaffold you are seeing a small part of that background, which is one reason to compare with other groups. Second, D is sensitive to how you filtered: discarding rare variants with a minor allele frequency cut-off removes exactly the variants that make D negative.

Pi per site

vcftools --vcf --site-pi

Pi per window

vcftools --vcf --window-pi
NotePi, per site and per window

Nucleotide diversity, π, is the average number of differences per base between two randomly chosen sequences from the population. Per site it is a small number computed at each variable position; per window it is averaged over a stretch of sequence, which is almost always the more useful view.

Window size is a choice you are making, not a detail. Too small and each window contains too few variants to estimate anything stably, so the plot is dominated by noise. Too large and a real signal is averaged away against its surroundings. Try more than one and see how much your conclusions depend on it — if a feature disappears when you change the window size, it was never there.

Watch out for one trap in particular: a window containing almost no callable sites can show spuriously high or low diversity. Always plot the number of variants per window alongside π, so you can see which points are supported by data.

Fst. Here, create a file of individuals from 6 populations (2 SWF, 2 NEF, Sweden, Poland) that you will use as pop1 and pop2 to do a pairwise Fst analysis to determine what the fixation index is. I have made a list of the individuals per population that you can find in the course repo on Github.

vcftools --vcf --weir-fst-pop pop1 --weir-fst-pop pop2 --fst-window-size
Warning

Note the two dashes on --fst-window-size. A single dash gives Error: Unknown option, and because VCFtools stops there, it is an easy one to stare past.

Each population file is a plain list of individual names, one per line, exactly as they are spelled in the VCF. Check them against bcftools query -l or the #CHROM header line rather than against the paper — a name that does not match a sample in the VCF is silently ignored, so a misspelling shows up as a population that is smaller than you think rather than as an error.

NoteReading an Fst value

Fst asks how much of the total genetic variation is explained by the division of individuals into populations. Zero means the populations are indistinguishable — all the variation is within them. One means they are fixed for different alleles, sharing nothing.

Real values for populations of a wind-dispersed forest fungus are usually small, and small is a finding rather than a failure: it means gene flow between the regions is high enough to keep them genetically similar. The paper this data comes from says as much in its title.

Three things to keep in your head while you interpret your numbers:

  • A mean Fst over your whole scaffold is a statement about the populations. An Fst outlier window is a statement about that region of the genome, and the two answer different questions.
  • Per-site Fst estimates are noisy, and individual sites can even come out slightly negative, which simply means no detectable differentiation. Windowed estimates are what you should interpret.
  • Fst depends on how much variation there is overall. A region of low diversity can show high Fst without any selection acting on it, which is why Fst outliers are usually interpreted alongside π and Tajima’s D rather than alone.
NoteRandom mating, and a statistic you cannot use here

The obvious next question is whether individuals within a population are mating at random, and the usual test is a comparison of observed and expected genotype frequencies under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium:

vcftools --vcf --hardy

Try it, and then think carefully about what it can possibly mean. Hardy–Weinberg describes how two alleles at a locus combine into genotypes — homozygotes and heterozygotes — in a randomly mating diploid population. Our fungus is haploid for most of its life cycle. It has one allele at each locus, so there are no genotypes to count and no heterozygote deficit to measure.

This is not a quirk of the software. It is a reminder that a statistic is a model of a process, and the model has to match the organism. The question “are these individuals mating at random?” is a good one for this fungus, but it has to be asked differently — through linkage disequilibrium, through the distribution of clonal genotypes, or through the structure analyses we do tomorrow.

For an organism where the test does apply, look back at the birch data from this morning: eleven diploid, outcrossing, wind-pollinated trees is exactly the situation Hardy–Weinberg was formulated for.

PCA that will also be plotted in R. For the PCA, you will plot the eigenvalues to illustrate what each component explains in your data, as well as PC1 and PC2, Pc2 and PC3, and PC3 and PC4.

plink --vcf --pca

If there is a region that is under selection within your data, have a look at the gff3 file to see the genes that are present within the region.

With these kinds of analyses, you are very well on the way to finding regions that are under selection by combining Tajima’s D and Fst. You can show which regions have higher SNP density.

NoteDiscussion
  1. Where did you set your depth and quality thresholds, and how many sites did each filter remove? Did any of them remove more than you expected?
  2. What is the mean Fst between your two populations? Is it small, and if so, what does that tell you about this species?
  3. Is Tajima’s D on your scaffold mostly positive, mostly negative, or centred on zero? Is that a statement about the population or about this region of the genome?
  4. Do your Tajima’s D and Fst outliers coincide? If a window stands out in both, what would you need in order to claim it is under selection — and what else could produce the same pattern?
  5. Compare with the group next to you. Does your scaffold look like theirs?
  6. Which of your filtering decisions would most change your answer to question 3 if you had chosen differently?
