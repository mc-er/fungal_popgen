Session 3: Quality Control and the VCF Format

Introduction

Yesterday afternoon we launched Sarek on eleven silver birch (Betula pendula) individuals from Salojärvi et al. (2017), with the reads subset to two chromosomes. This morning we find out what it did.

This session is different from the others. There are commands to run, but the point of the morning is not the commands — it is judgement. You will be asked to look at a set of quality metrics and decide whether the data behind them can carry the conclusions we want to draw this afternoon. That decision is yours to make and yours to defend, and there is no green tick anywhere that makes it for you.

NotePlan for the morning
  1. Find the output and confirm the pipeline actually finished
  2. Read the FastQC reports: what the modules mean, and what they mean for our data
  3. Use MultiQC to compare all samples at once and find the ones that are not like the others
  4. Look at alignment and coverage, where working on a non-model organism starts to show
  5. Open the VCF and learn to read it
  6. Decide, as a group, what we are going to filter on this afternoon

Expect to spend roughly half the morning on QC and half on the VCF, with discussion throughout.

And as always: don’t forget to ask questions if something does not behave the way you expect.

Finding the output

Sarek writes its results into the directory you gave it with --outdir:

results
├── csv                 # samplesheets describing the output files
├── multiqc             # the aggregated report - start here
├── pipeline_info       # Nextflow's own execution reports
├── preprocessing       # BAM/CRAM files
├── reference           # indexes built from our reference
├── reports             # per-tool QC output
│   ├── fastqc
│   ├── mosdepth
│   ├── samtools
│   ├── bcftools
│   └── vcftools
└── variant_calling     # the VCFs

First, did it finish? Nextflow prints a summary at the end of a run, and writes the same information to the execution report:

ls results/pipeline_info/

Open the execution report in a browser. It tells you how many tasks succeeded, how many failed, how long the run took, and how much memory each process actually used.

Important

A pipeline that ends without an error message is not the same as a pipeline that did what you wanted. Check that the number of samples in the output matches the number of samples in your samplesheet. Missing samples fail quietly more often than you would like.

ls results/reports/fastqc/ | wc -l
wc -l samplesheet.csv

Opening HTML reports

The QC reports are HTML files on the cluster, and you need a browser to read them. Three ways, in order of convenience:

  1. Start a session through Open OnDemand and open the file from the Files pane — this is what we set up on Tuesday
  2. Open the file in VS Code with a preview extension
  3. Copy it to your laptop with scp and open it locally
# from your laptop
scp -r <username>@kebnekaise.hpc2n.umu.se:/proj/.../results/multiqc .
Tip

MultiQC reports are self-contained single HTML files, so copying one to your laptop gives you a report that still works offline. FastQC reports are the same. This is a good format to send to a collaborator, or to attach to a thesis appendix.

Part 1: FastQC

FastQC runs on the raw reads, before any trimming, and produces one report per FASTQ file — so two per sample per lane, one for R1 and one for R2.

ls results/reports/fastqc/

Open one and look at it before reading any further. Then come back.

ImportantThe single most important thing about FastQC

The green ticks, amber exclamation marks and red crosses are not verdicts on your data.

FastQC compares your file against what it expects from a random, diverse, unbiased library sequenced on a well-behaved instrument. It knows nothing about your experiment, your organism, or your library preparation. A red cross means “this is unusual for an idealised library” — which for many perfectly good datasets is simply true.

Two consequences. A red cross is an instruction to investigate, not to discard. And a column of green ticks is not evidence that your data is fit for purpose.

The modules, and what they mean for us

Module What it shows What to expect in our data
Basic Statistics Read count, read length, %GC Sanity check. Wildly different read counts between samples matter later
Per base sequence quality Quality score by position along the read Gradual decline toward the 3’ end is normal. A cliff is not
Per tile sequence quality Quality by physical position on the flowcell Localised problems — a bubble, a smudge. Usually nothing you can act on
Per sequence quality scores Distribution of mean read quality A second peak at low quality means a subpopulation of bad reads
Per base sequence content %A/%C/%G/%T by position Bias in the first ~10 bases is normal and expected (see below)
Per sequence GC content GC distribution across reads Watch this one. A second peak suggests another organism
Per base N content Uncalled bases by position Should be flat and near zero
Sequence Length Distribution Read lengths Uniform before trimming
Sequence Duplication Levels How often reads are seen more than once High duplication means low library complexity
Overrepresented sequences Individual sequences seen often Adapters, rRNA, or contamination. The table names what it recognises
Adapter Content Adapter sequence by position Rises toward the 3’ end when inserts are short. Trimming deals with it

The four worth your attention

Per base sequence quality. Quality is reported on the Phred scale, which is logarithmic:

Phred score Probability the base is wrong Accuracy
Q10 1 in 10 90%
Q20 1 in 100 99%
Q30 1 in 1000 99.9%
Q40 1 in 10 000 99.99%

Modern Illumina data sits around Q30–Q36 for most of the read and falls off at the end. That tail is why we trim. What should worry you is a sudden collapse, a read that starts badly, or one sample that looks quite different from the rest.

Per base sequence content. The first several bases almost always show a strong, repeatable bias. This is a well-known artefact of the random priming used in library preparation, not a problem with your sample, and FastQC flags it on nearly every genomic library ever made. What is not normal is bias continuing along the whole read, which suggests a composition problem or heavy contamination.

Per sequence GC content. This is the module to look at hardest, and the reason is biological. The DNA came from the tissue of a tree growing outdoors, and a tree is a habitat: endophytic fungi, epiphytes, bacteria and insects all live in and on it, and whatever came along in the sample was sequenced too. Because different organisms have different GC content, a contaminant shows up as a second peak or a conspicuous shoulder beside the expected unimodal distribution.

Plant material adds a second, entirely expected source of structure here: chloroplasts and mitochondria are present in many copies per cell and have their own GC content, so organellar reads can form a distinct mode of their own.

Warning

Contamination is not a hypothetical concern for field-collected plant material; it is routine. It also does not announce itself later in the pipeline: contaminant reads either fail to map, which looks like a low mapping rate, or map to repetitive and conserved regions, which looks like variants. A bimodal GC distribution now is much easier to deal with than a strange population structure plot on Thursday.

Sequence Duplication Levels. Duplicates arise when the same original DNA fragment is sequenced more than once, usually because PCR amplified it during library preparation. They matter for us because they inflate apparent read depth without adding information: ten reads that are all copies of one fragment look like tenfold coverage but carry one fragment’s worth of evidence. This is exactly why the pipeline marks duplicates before calling variants.

NoteExercise: read one report properly

Pick one sample and work through its FastQC report with a neighbour. Write down:

  1. How many reads, and how long are they?
  2. At which position does the mean quality drop below Q30, if it does?
  3. Is the GC distribution unimodal? What is the modal GC content?
  4. What fraction of reads is flagged as duplicates?
  5. Is there adapter signal, and from which position?
  6. Which modules got a red cross, and for each one: is it a problem, or is it FastQC being FastQC?

Question 6 is the one we will discuss as a group.

Part 2: MultiQC

With eleven trees and two read files each, that is twenty-two FastQC reports, and nobody opens twenty-two of anything. MultiQC collects the output of every tool in the pipeline, for every sample, into one interactive report.

ls results/multiqc/
# multiqc_report.html   multiqc_data/   multiqc_plots/
ImportantThe single most important thing about MultiQC

Your samples are each other’s controls.

With one sample, you can only compare against a general expectation, and general expectations are weak when you work on a non-model organism. With twenty samples prepared and sequenced the same way, you have something much stronger: anything that happened to all of them is probably biology or protocol, and anything that happened to one of them is probably a problem with that one.

So do not read the report sample by sample. Read it column by column, looking for the ones that are not like the others.

How to work through the report

  1. General Statistics, at the top, is a table with one row per sample. Click a column header to sort by it. Sort by % duplication, then % GC, then median coverage, then % mapped, and each time look at the top and bottom of the list. The same sample appearing at the bad end of several columns is your outlier.
  2. FastQC section. The status heatmap gives you all samples by all modules at a glance. The curve plots overlay every sample, so an odd one is visible as a line that does not follow the bundle.
  3. Alignment, from samtools stats and mosdepth: mapping rate, error rate, insert size distribution, coverage.
  4. Variants, from bcftools stats and VCFtools: variant counts, depth distribution, transition/transversion ratio.
  5. The toolbox on the right lets you highlight a sample in a colour across every plot in the report. Highlight your suspected outlier and scroll through — it is the fastest way to see whether one sample is consistently odd or just unlucky in one metric.
NoteExercise: find the outliers

Working in your pair, use the General Statistics table to identify the three samples you are least happy with. For each one, write a single sentence of the form:

Sample X is unusual because [metric] is [value] compared with [typical value across samples], which could be caused by [hypothesis].

Then decide, for each: keep it, keep it with a note, or drop it. Be ready to defend the decision — in particular, be ready to say what would change your mind.

This is not a trick question with a right answer. It is the actual decision that gets made in every sequencing project, usually with less discussion than it deserves.

Part 3: Alignment and coverage

Here is where working on a non-model organism becomes visible in the numbers.

Mapping rate and reference bias

Our reference genome was assembled from one birch tree — and not one of ours. The reference individual, dhBetPend1, was collected in Surrey in the United Kingdom; our eleven trees are Finnish, some two thousand kilometres away. It is also a haplotype-resolved assembly, so the reference is one of that tree’s two haplotypes.

Every other tree differs from the reference, and the more an individual differs, the harder its reads are to map. Birch is wind-pollinated and outcrossing, so individuals carry a great deal of variation relative to any single reference, and a reference from the far end of the species’ range is a long way from all of our samples. For human data a mapping rate above 95% is routine, because the reference is a well-polished consensus of the species. Do not expect that here, and do not treat a lower number as failure.

What matters is the comparison across samples. A sample with a markedly lower mapping rate than the rest is telling you something: it may be more divergent, it may be contaminated, or it may not be the species you think it is.

WarningReference bias

Reads carrying the reference allele map slightly more easily than reads carrying an alternative allele, because they match the reference better. The effect is small per read and systematic across the dataset, and it pushes estimated allele frequencies toward the reference.

That matters for us specifically, because almost everything we do this week is built on allele frequencies: Tajima’s D, Fst, the site frequency spectrum, the PCA. A bias of this kind does not produce an error or an obviously wrong plot. It produces a slightly wrong answer that looks entirely reasonable.

It also does not fall equally on all samples. Trees closely related to whichever individual the reference came from are affected less than distant ones — so a bias in mapping can masquerade as a signal of population structure. Hold on to this one; it is worth raising again on Thursday when the structure plots appear.

Coverage

Mosdepth reports how deeply each part of the genome was sequenced:

ls results/reports/mosdepth/
cat results/reports/mosdepth/<sample>/*.mosdepth.summary.txt

Three things to look at:

  • Mean and median depth per sample. These should be broadly similar across samples. If one tree has half the depth of the others, its genotypes are less certain, and it will have more missing data after filtering. Be aware that our eleven samples were not sequenced equally: read counts in the published data range from 32.6 to 56.2 million pairs, a spread of about 1.7-fold, so some difference in depth is expected before you even start.
  • The cumulative coverage distribution, in MultiQC. It answers the question you actually care about: what fraction of the genome is covered at at least N× in this sample?
  • Coverage per contig. Our subset contains only two chromosomes, so there is not much to compare — but note what you would look at in a full dataset. The full assembly includes a mitochondrion (744 kb) and a chloroplast (161 kb), and if organellar sequences are in your reference they are covered far more deeply than the nuclear genome, because a cell contains one nucleus and many mitochondria and chloroplasts. Collapsed repeats show up as unusually deep. And a whole scaffold with conspicuously different coverage in some individuals than others is worth investigating rather than ignoring: in plants that can mean a large structural variant, and it can also mean the sample is not the species you think it is.
NoteHow much coverage is enough?

There is no single number, because it depends on what you are asking.

Calling a genotype confidently at one site in one individual needs enough reads to rule out sampling error, and in a diploid that bar is higher than you might think. A homozygote is easy: every read agrees. A heterozygote requires you to see both alleles, and each read samples one chromosome at random — so at 4× coverage there is a one-in-eight chance that all four reads come from the same chromosome and a true heterozygote is called homozygous.

That error is not random with respect to what we measure. Missing heterozygotes systematically depresses estimated heterozygosity and distorts allele frequencies, and it does so more in the samples with the lowest coverage — which is exactly why you checked whether depth varies between samples before comparing them.

But many population-genetic statistics are computed across thousands of sites and many individuals, and they tolerate uncertainty at any single site better than you might expect. This is why low-coverage sequencing of many individuals is often a better use of a budget than high-coverage sequencing of a few — you learn more about a population from 50 individuals at 5× than from 5 individuals at 50×.

What is not acceptable is coverage that varies systematically with something you intend to compare. If one group of trees was sequenced more deeply than another, then any difference you find between those groups has an alternative explanation that has nothing to do with biology. Check this now, before you do the comparison — and remember that our samples already differ by about 1.7-fold in read count.

Part 4: The VCF

Everything above was about whether to trust the data. Now we look at the data itself.

ls results/variant_calling/haplotypecaller/joint_variant_calling/
Warning

You may find two VCFs here: joint_germline.vcf.gz and joint_germline_recalibrated.vcf.gz. The second has had GATK’s variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) applied, which is a machine-learning filter trained on curated sets of known true variants.

Those training sets exist for humans. They do not exist for birch. Use the unrecalibrated joint_germline.vcf.gz, and filter it ourselves on explicit, defensible thresholds this afternoon — which is what we would have to do anyway for any non-model organism.

Looking at a VCF without regretting it

A VCF is a plain text file, usually compressed with bgzip. It is also potentially enormous. Do not open one in a text editor, and never in Excel.

VCF=results/variant_calling/haplotypecaller/joint_variant_calling/joint_germline.vcf.gz

bcftools view -h $VCF | less -S     # the header only
bcftools view -H $VCF | head -3     # the first few records, no header
bcftools query -l $VCF              # the sample names
bcftools view -H $VCF | wc -l       # how many records
Tip

less -S stops lines from wrapping, which makes a wide file readable: use the arrow keys to scroll sideways, and q to quit. Piping to head is safe even on a huge file — the pipe closes and bcftools stops.

The CHROM values in your file are whatever the sequence names in the reference FASTA are, which for our assembly means the chromosomes are named 1 to 14 — though a subset may have been renamed. Check rather than assume, with cut -f1 reference.fasta.fai or bcftools view -h $VCF | grep contig. A mismatch between what you type in a region query and what is actually in the file returns nothing at all, with no error.

Anatomy of a VCF

Every VCF has two parts: header lines beginning with ##, then one line per variant site. Here is a small, complete example:

##fileformat=VCFv4.2
##FILTER=<ID=PASS,Description="All filters passed">
##contig=<ID=chr1,length=250000>
##INFO=<ID=DP,Number=1,Type=Integer,Description="Approximate read depth">
##INFO=<ID=AF,Number=A,Type=Float,Description="Allele Frequency">
##INFO=<ID=AC,Number=A,Type=Integer,Description="Allele count in genotypes">
##INFO=<ID=AN,Number=1,Type=Integer,Description="Total number of alleles">
##INFO=<ID=MQ,Number=1,Type=Float,Description="RMS Mapping Quality">
##FORMAT=<ID=GT,Number=1,Type=String,Description="Genotype">
##FORMAT=<ID=AD,Number=R,Type=Integer,Description="Allelic depths">
##FORMAT=<ID=DP,Number=1,Type=Integer,Description="Read depth">
##FORMAT=<ID=GQ,Number=1,Type=Integer,Description="Genotype Quality">
##FORMAT=<ID=PL,Number=G,Type=Integer,Description="Phred-scaled likelihoods">
#CHROM  POS ID  REF ALT QUAL    FILTER  INFO    FORMAT  SAMEA104151691  SAMEA104151857  SAMEA104151870
chr1    1420    .   A   G   982.4   PASS    DP=71;AF=0.333;AC=2;AN=6;MQ=60.00   GT:AD:DP:GQ:PL  0/0:24,0:24:72:0,72,1080    1/1:0,19:19:57:855,57,0 0/0:28,0:28:84:0,84,1260
chr1    1655    .   C   T   45.2    PASS    DP=19;AF=0.167;AC=1;AN=6;MQ=41.00   GT:AD:DP:GQ:PL  0/1:5,4:9:38:38,0,112   0/0:6,0:6:18:0,18,270   ./.:0,0:0:.:.
chr1    2310    .   G   GAT 721.9   PASS    DP=66;AF=0.500;AC=3;AN=6;MQ=59.00   GT:AD:DP:GQ:PL  1/1:0,22:22:66:990,66,0 0/0:21,0:21:63:0,63,945 1/1:0,23:23:69:1035,69,0

The header is not decoration. It is the file’s own documentation: every INFO and FORMAT field used in the records below is defined there, with its type and a description. When you meet an unfamiliar field — and you will — the definition is in the header of the file in front of you.

bcftools view -h $VCF | grep "^##INFO"

The last header line, the one with a single #, is the column header for everything that follows.

The columns

# Column What it holds
1 CHROM Chromosome or scaffold name
2 POS Position, 1-based, of the first base of REF
3 ID Variant identifier, e.g. an rs number. . when there is none, which for us is always
4 REF The reference allele
5 ALT The alternative allele(s), comma-separated if more than one
6 QUAL Phred-scaled confidence that there is a variant here at all
7 FILTER PASS, or the names of the filters this site failed
8 INFO Semicolon-separated annotations describing the site
9 FORMAT Which per-sample fields follow, and in what order
10+ one per sample The per-sample values, colon-separated, in FORMAT order

Columns 1–8 describe the site. Column 9 onwards describe the individuals. That division is the whole structure of the format, and it is worth saying out loud: everything from column 10 to the end is one individual’s data.

NoteSNPs, indels and positions

Look at the third record in the example. REF is G and ALT is GAT, which is an insertion of two bases after position 2310 — not a substitution.

This is why POS alone does not tell you how long a variant is, and why --remove-indels is one of the first things we do this afternoon. Most population-genetic statistics are defined for single nucleotide polymorphisms, and indels in a short-read alignment are considerably less reliable than substitutions.

INFO: fields that describe the site

Field Meaning Why you care
DP Total read depth at the site, summed over samples Very low depth means little evidence; very high depth often means a collapsed repeat
AF Allele frequency of the alternative allele The quantity most of this week is built on
AC / AN Allele count and total alleles called AC/AN is the frequency computed from actual calls
MQ Root-mean-square mapping quality of the reads Low MQ means the reads here could have come from somewhere else
QD Quality normalised by depth Depth-independent confidence, better for filtering than raw QUAL
FS, SOR Strand bias measures A real variant should appear on both strands
Tip

Extremely high DP is as suspicious as low DP. A site with five times the average depth is usually a region where two or more similar copies of a sequence have collapsed into one in the assembly, so reads from several places pile onto one location. These sites produce beautiful, confident, false variants, and they are a particular hazard in fungal genomes, which are full of repeat families and duplicated gene clusters.

This is why we will set an upper depth bound this afternoon, not just a lower one.

FORMAT: fields that describe each individual

FORMAT says GT:AD:DP:GQ:PL, so in each sample column the colon-separated values are in that order.

Field Meaning
GT Genotype
AD Reads supporting each allele, reference first
DP Read depth for this individual at this site
GQ Phred-scaled confidence in this genotype call
PL Phred-scaled likelihoods of each possible genotype, smallest is the call

The genotype encoding uses indices into REF and ALT: 0 is the reference allele, 1 is the first alternative, 2 the second, and so on.

GT Meaning
0/0 Homozygous reference
0/1 Heterozygous
1/1 Homozygous alternative
./. Missing — no call could be made
0 or 1 A haploid call, with a single allele. Not in this dataset, but you will meet these in the fungal VCF this afternoon
0\|1 Phased, with a known parental origin. We will not see these

Take the second record and read 0/1:5,4:9:38:38,0,112 out loud: heterozygous, 5 reads supporting the reference and 4 the alternative, 9 reads in total, genotype quality 38, and the likelihoods say the heterozygous call is best — but only modestly better than homozygous reference. Compare it with the first record’s 0/0:24,0:24:72:0,72,1080, where 24 of 24 reads agree. Both are calls. They are not equally trustworthy, and nothing but the numbers tells you so.

WarningHeterozygotes and allele balance

Silver birch is diploid and outcrossing, so heterozygous calls are not a warning sign here — they are most of the signal. What you check instead is whether each heterozygous call is internally consistent, and AD is how you do it.

At a true heterozygous site, each read samples one of the two chromosomes at random, so the two AD values should be roughly equal. The call 0/1:5,4:9:38:38,0,112 has 5 reads against 4, which is exactly what a real heterozygote looks like. A call like 0/1:28,3:31:20:... does not: 28 reads against 3 is not a coin flip, and something other than two alleles at one locus is generating it.

Strong allele imbalance, especially in combination with high depth, usually means one of:

  • Collapsed repeats or paralogues — reads from two similar regions of the genome piled onto one location, so the “alleles” are really two different loci
  • Contamination — a few reads from another organism or another individual
  • Sequencing error, particularly at low depth

The per-sample heterozygosity in the MultiQC variant section is worth a look for the same reason. A tree with far more heterozygous calls than the others may be an artefact, but in birch it may also be biology.

NoteWhy that last point is not hypothetical in birch

Silver birch, B. pendula, is diploid with 2n = 2x = 28. Downy birch, B. pubescens, grows in the same forests, resembles it closely enough that the two are regularly confused in the field — and is tetraploid. The two species also hybridise, and introgression between them is documented.

So an unusual pattern of heterozygosity in one sample is a question worth taking seriously rather than filtering away: it could be a collapsed repeat, or it could be telling you something about what that tree actually is. “Check that the sample is the species you think it is” belongs on every QC checklist for wild-collected material — and it is a check nobody can do for you after the fact.

Hands-on

VCF=results/variant_calling/haplotypecaller/joint_variant_calling/joint_germline.vcf.gz

# How many samples, and what are they called?
bcftools query -l $VCF

# How many variant records in total?
bcftools view -H $VCF | wc -l

# How many are SNPs, as opposed to indels?
bcftools view -H -v snps $VCF | wc -l

# Pull out selected fields as a table
bcftools query -f '%CHROM\t%POS\t%REF\t%ALT\t%QUAL\t%INFO/DP\n' $VCF | head

# The same, with each sample's genotype and depth
bcftools query -f '%POS[\t%SAMPLE=%GT:%DP]\n' $VCF | head

# Everything in one region
bcftools view -H $VCF chr1:1-50000 | cut -f1,2,4,5,6

# A summary of the whole file
bcftools stats $VCF | less -S
NoteExercise: interrogate the VCF
  1. How many variants are in the file, and how many samples?
  2. What fraction are indels rather than SNPs?
  3. What is the median QUAL? (Hint: bcftools query, then sort -n.)
  4. Find a site where at least one sample is ./.. Why might the call be missing there?
  5. Find the site with the highest INFO/DP in the first 100 000 bases. Would you trust it?
  6. For one sample, count how many genotypes are heterozygous. Then find a heterozygous call whose AD values are badly unbalanced. Would you trust it?
  7. What does bcftools stats report for the transition/transversion ratio?

Question 7 deserves a comment. Ts/Tv is a standard sanity check: transitions are chemically easier than transversions, so real variation shows an excess of transitions, while random sequencing errors do not. In humans the expected value is well established, around 2 for whole genomes. For birch nobody has told us what to expect — so use it as a relative measure. Compute it before and after filtering, and if filtering raises it, you removed proportionally more noise than signal.

Part 5: Deciding what to do about it

The point of the morning is the handover to this afternoon. Before we break, we decide together on the filtering thresholds we would apply, and why.

Important

One thing to be clear about: this afternoon we change dataset. The birch data you have just assessed is a diploid tree, sequenced whole-genome, and we looked at it to learn how to read pipeline output. For the population statistics you will work on a VCF from a forest fungus instead, with one chromosome assigned to each pair, because it is haploid, smaller, and comes with the population metadata that the statistics need.

What transfers between the two is not the numbers but the reasoning. The thresholds we agree on now are the ones you will justify, adapt and defend on a different file in an hour — which is a fair description of how this works in practice, since you will rarely assess the same dataset twice.

NoteDiscussion
  1. Which samples, if any, are we dropping, and on what grounds? What is our rule, stated so that someone else could apply it to the same data and reach the same decision?
  2. What minimum depth will we require for a genotype call? What maximum, and why is there a maximum at all?
  3. What minimum quality? Raw QUAL, or QD?
  4. How much missing data will we tolerate at a site before we discard the site? How much for an individual?
  5. This VCF came from a diploid organism called with a diploid model, so the genotypes mean what they say. The fungal VCF we move to this afternoon comes from a haploid organism, and some of the statistics we will calculate are defined in terms of diploid genotype counts. What has to change, and what breaks if it does not?
  6. Every filter removes real variants as well as artefacts. Which direction does each of our filters push the statistics we are about to calculate? A filter on minimum allele frequency, for instance, removes rare variants — and Tajima’s D is a statement about exactly those.
  7. If this were your project, what would the methods paragraph say? Write three sentences.
Tip

Write the agreed thresholds down before lunch, with the reason for each next to it. You will adapt them to the fungal data this afternoon, you will want them for your report, and in a real project you will want them in six months when a reviewer asks why the numbers are what they are.
